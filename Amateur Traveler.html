Hear about travel to Madrid, Spain, as the Amateur Traveler talks to Felicity Hughes of TheMakingOfMadrid.com about how to spend about a week exploring her adopted hometown.

Felicity moved to Madrid in 2012, writes and podcasts about the city, and is currently the lead writer for Lonely Planet’s Madrid guides. She recommends giving Madrid more than the typical three-day visit. While you can see the headline sights quickly, a week gives you time to explore Madrid’s historic neighborhoods, museums, food culture, and parks, while also taking two important day trips to Toledo and El Escorial.

Why should you go to Madrid?

Felicity says, “I think it’s brilliant… Madrid has London beat… the transport system here rarely breaks down. It’s really efficient, it’s really cheap… And the people… everybody’s really accepting of you… They’re so friendly and open. And of course, the glorious sunny weather. For me, the essential charm of Madrid is the people… you won’t get to experience that unless you take a breath and just sit in a bar and have a conversation with someone… People are so friendly here, it’s incredible.”

Day 1 – Historic and Royal Madrid

Start in Puerta del Sol, the symbolic heart of Madrid and one of the city’s main transportation hubs. Look for the statue of the Bear and the Strawberry Tree, Madrid’s heraldic symbol, as well as Kilómetro Cero, the point from which Spain’s national roads are traditionally measured.

Walk from Sol to the Plaza Mayor, the grand square developed after Philip II moved his court to Madrid in 1561. Over the centuries, the square has hosted markets, bullfights, festivals, and even trials connected with the Spanish Inquisition.

Continue along Calle Mayor toward Plaza de la Villa, where some of Madrid’s oldest surviving buildings surround the former medieval town square. Felicity points out architectural traces of Madrid’s Islamic past, including Mudéjar details left by Muslim craftsmen.

Nearby is the Mercado de San Miguel, a beautiful iron-and-glass market hall. Felicity recommends visiting before about 11 a.m. or late at night to avoid the heaviest crowds. For a more local market experience, she suggests exploring markets elsewhere in Madrid, including Mercado de Antón Martín in Lavapiés.

From Plaza de la Villa, find the appropriately named Calle del Codo, or “Elbow Street,” and visit the Convent of the Carboneras, where cloistered nuns sell traditional cookies through a turntable. Bring cash and a little Spanish can be useful.

Continue toward Madrid’s royal quarter. Felicity suggests not necessarily spending your first day touring the Royal Palace, since a visit can easily take two hours or more. Instead, explore the area around the palace and cathedral first.

Near the Almudena Cathedral, look down into the Parque del Emir Mohamed I, where you can see remnants of Madrid’s original ninth-century Muslim walls and get a sense of where the city began.

If you do visit the Royal Palace of Madrid, Felicity recommends getting the audio guide because the rooms themselves contain relatively little interpretive information. She is especially enthusiastic about the recently renovated Royal Armoury, where elaborate suits of armor include pieces made for Charles V and even armor for his hunting dog.

An alternative, and one of Felicity’s strongest recommendations, is the nearby Galería de las Colecciones Reales, the Gallery of the Royal Collections. The museum contains works by artists including Velázquez, Caravaggio, and Goya while providing considerably more historical context about Spain’s royal dynasties than the palace itself.

From the gallery, descend into the Campo del Moro, the landscaped palace gardens filled with peacocks and views toward the Sierra de Guadarrama. Felicity calls this one of her favorite places in Madrid.

Day 2 – Day Trip to Toledo

Felicity recommends visiting Toledo early in the itinerary because it helps explain the medieval history that largely disappeared beneath later development in Madrid.

Madrid eventually eclipsed Toledo as Spain’s political center, but Toledo preserved extraordinary layers of Christian, Jewish, and Islamic history.

Rather than taking the train, Felicity slightly prefers the bus from Estación de Plaza Elíptica. The bus arrives near escalators that carry visitors uphill into Toledo’s historic center, avoiding part of the climb from the train station.

Highlights include:

  • Mezquita del Cristo de la Luz, a former mosque later converted into a church
  • Synagogue of Santa María la Blanca
  • Synagogue of El Tránsito
  • Monastery of San Juan de los Reyes
  • El Greco Museum
  • Toledo’s shops specializing in marzipan

The city’s Muslim, Jewish, and Christian heritage earned Toledo a reputation as the “City of Three Cultures,” and its historic center is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Day Trip – El Escorial

Felicity’s second essential excursion is San Lorenzo de El Escorial, the massive palace, monastery, basilica, library, and royal burial complex built for Philip II.

Take the bus from Moncloa, which drops you much closer to El Escorial than the train station at the bottom of the hill. Felicity also recommends bringing a picnic and arriving early. There is enough to see here to spend four or five hours.

Highlights include the magnificent Renaissance library, the basilica, extensive royal art collections, the Hall of Battles, and the extraordinary royal crypt containing generations of Spanish monarchs.

El Escorial also provides important context for understanding Madrid because Philip II was the monarch who established his court in Madrid and effectively transformed it into Spain’s capital.

Explore La Latina

Back in Madrid, spend time in La Latina, one of the oldest and most atmospheric parts of the city.

Start with the free Museum of the Origins of Madrid, also known as the Museum of San Isidro. Among its exhibits is the well associated with Madrid’s patron saint, San Isidro.

If you visit around May 15, Madrid celebrates the Festival of San Isidro. Madrileños dress in traditional chulapo and chulapa costumes, complete with carnations whose colors traditionally indicate whether the wearer is single, married, or engaged.

Then simply wander La Latina’s medieval street plan.

Felicity particularly recommends Plaza de la Paja and the small Jardines del Príncipe de Anglona, one of the quiet corners she highlights for travelers looking for a break from Madrid’s busier attractions. Nearby is the Plaza de la Cruz Verde, historically associated with the Spanish Inquisition but today a much more peaceful square.

Finish the day at the Jardines de las Vistillas, one of Felicity’s favorite viewpoints, with views toward the Casa de Campo and Sierra de Guadarrama mountains.

For an evening of flamenco, Felicity recommends Corral de la Morería, in the former Moorish quarter, which combines flamenco performances with highly regarded food.

Temple of Debod and Sunset

Another good sunset destination is the Temple of Debod, an ancient Egyptian temple given to Spain after Spanish archaeologists helped preserve monuments threatened by construction of the Aswan High Dam.

Admission is free, but Felicity recommends booking entrance well in advance, particularly if you want a sunset time slot. The surrounding viewpoint looks across the Casa de Campo toward the Royal Palace, which often turns pink in the evening light.

Madrid’s Great Art Museums

Dedicate another day to Madrid’s famous Triangle of Art.

The Prado Museum is the obvious centerpiece, with masterpieces including Bosch’s Garden of Earthly Delights, Velázquez’s Las Meninas, and Goya’s disturbing Black Paintings. Felicity recommends arriving early because the museum is becoming increasingly crowded.

One useful strategy is buying the approximately €32 art pass, which includes admission to:

  • Prado Museum
  • Reina Sofía
  • Thyssen-Bornemisza Museum

The Reina Sofía is home to Picasso’s Guernica, while the Thyssen-Bornemisza covers a much broader span of art history and is Felicity’s recommendation for travelers who want a great museum experience with fewer crowds.

Retiro Park

After the museums, spend time in Retiro Park, originally part of a royal palace complex associated with Philip IV.

Much of the original palace was destroyed during the Napoleonic era, but the grounds eventually became a public park and remain one of Madrid’s loveliest places for a walk.

The famous Palacio de Cristal is currently closed for restoration and, according to Felicity, is expected to reopen in 2027, although the exact timing remains uncertain. There is still plenty to see throughout the park.

Explore Madrid’s Neighborhoods

Felicity stresses that a visit to Madrid should not consist entirely of major attractions.

Spend time exploring Barrio de las Letras, Madrid’s literary neighborhood associated with figures including Cervantes and Lope de Vega, as well as later visitors such as Hemingway and Virginia Woolf.

Also wander Malasaña, home to the Museum of the History of Madrid, which covers the city’s history from the period when Madrid became the royal capital into the modern era.

Most importantly, leave time to sit in a neighborhood bar. Felicity says Madrid’s friendliness and openness are among the city’s greatest attractions and something travelers can miss if they spend the entire trip rushing from one landmark to another.

What to Eat in Madrid

Try cocido madrileño, Madrid’s traditional chickpea-and-meat stew served in stages, starting with broth and noodles followed by chickpeas, vegetables, and meats.

Another Madrid specialty is callos a la madrileña, a rich tripe stew.

For something quicker, order a bocadillo de calamares, a fried calamari sandwich traditionally eaten around the Plaza Mayor. Felicity recommends La Campana or Ideal.

And, of course, leave room for churros dipped in thick hot chocolate and perhaps a glass of madroño liqueur, made from the fruit represented on Madrid’s famous bear-and-tree symbol.
